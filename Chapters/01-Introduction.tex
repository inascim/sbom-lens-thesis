\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Modern software is rarely built from scratch. The overwhelming majority of production systems depend on third-party open-source components: the Synopsys 2024 Open Source Security and Risk Analysis report found that 96\% of commercial codebases contain open-source code and that the average codebase incorporates over 500 open-source components \citep{Synopsys2024OSSRA}. Many of these components arrive as dependencies of other components, forming a supply chain whose code is several times larger than the first-party code that developers directly author. The software supply chain --- the aggregate of all such components, their origins, build pipelines, and trust relationships --- has consequently become one of the most consequential and least understood risk surfaces in modern cybersecurity.

The fragility of this supply chain was exposed in dramatic fashion by a succession of high-profile incidents. In December 2020, the SUNBURST campaign targeted SolarWinds, a network management vendor trusted by thousands of organisations including agencies of the United States federal government. Attackers compromised the vendor's build pipeline and inserted malicious code into a signed software update that was subsequently distributed to its customers \citep{CISA_AA20_352A}. The incident demonstrated how a single trusted upstream vendor can become an unwitting vector for cascading compromise across an entire downstream ecosystem. A year later, in December 2021, the disclosure of CVE-2021-44228 in Apache Log4j --- universally known as \textit{log4shell} --- revealed a critical remote code execution vulnerability in a logging library present, often as a transitive dependency, across the Java ecosystem \citep{NIST_CVE_2021_44228, GoogleLog4j2021}. The difficulty organisations faced in determining whether they were exposed illustrates precisely the challenge this thesis addresses: without a structural model of the dependency graph, an organisation may see that a vulnerable transitive dependency is present, but not how it entered the system or what depends on it. In March 2024, the xz-utils backdoor (CVE-2024-3094, CVSS 10.0) added a further dimension of supply chain risk to the record: a patient, multi-year social engineering campaign against an open-source maintainer resulted in the insertion of a cryptographic backdoor into a core Linux compression utility that, had it reached widespread deployment, would have compromised SSH authentication on affected systems \citep{NVD_CVE_2024_3094}. In September 2025, a self-replicating worm known as Shai-Hulud spread through the npm registry, using compromised maintainer accounts to publish malicious versions of more than 500 packages \citep{CISA2025npm}. Together, these four incidents illustrate that supply chain risks are diverse in method and severe in potential impact.

Regulators have responded by demanding greater transparency over software composition. In the European Union, the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), entered into force in December 2024, requires manufacturers and developers of products with digital elements sold in the EU market to document, from December 2027, the components of their software and to address known vulnerabilities throughout the product lifecycle \citep{CRA2024}. The CRA explicitly names the SBOM --- a formal record of a product's components and their supply chain relationships, which must cover at least its top-level dependencies --- as a key instrument of this transparency obligation. In the United States, Executive Order 14028, signed in May 2021, made the SBOM part of federal guidance on software supply chain security, defining it as ``a formal record containing the details and supply chain relationships of various components used in building software'' \citep{EO14028}. Since January 2026, however, each federal agency decides whether to require an SBOM from its vendors \citep{OMB_M2605}. The SBOM is thus becoming a legal requirement in the EU and remains an expected practice in US federal procurement, and the volume of SBOMs in circulation is likely to grow.

Yet the emergence of the SBOM as a transparency artefact has not been matched by commensurate progress in analytical tooling. Among the open-source tools in the SBOM ecosystem, Dependency-Track, grype and syft treat SBOMs essentially as component inventories, and Sunshine, which visualises the dependency tree, computes nothing over it beyond reachability \citep{DependencyTrack, Grype, Syft, Sunshine}. They answer two questions effectively: \textit{what components are present?} and \textit{do any of them have known CVEs?} What they do not do is model the structure of the dependency graph. When a vulnerability is disclosed in a given component, these tools report the affected component largely in isolation. Only Sunshine lists the answer to the question a security analyst immediately needs --- \textit{which other components in my system depend on this one, directly or transitively, and are therefore also at risk?} --- and it gives that answer as a table, not on the dependency graph. Nor do they identify which components, if compromised, would expose the largest number of dependents --- all the components that transitively rely on them and would therefore inherit the risk. The result is that analysts must manually triage long flat lists of CVEs with no structural context to guide prioritisation.

The opportunity for a more rigorous approach lies in the observation that a software dependency graph is, by construction, a directed graph --- and graph theory provides precisely the tools needed to answer both questions above. Given a directed dependency graph, the set of all components affected by a vulnerability in component \(v\) is \(v\) together with its ancestors in the graph, computable in linear time, and the dominator tree identifies the components that the application reaches only through \(v\) \citep{LengauerTarjan1979}. Graph-theoretic centrality measures further identify the components on which much of the dependency graph rests --- components that a security analyst may want to examine whatever their individual CVSS score. Articulation points identify components whose removal would split the graph, representing single points of structural failure. These techniques are well-established in graph theory \citep{CLRS4th, Newman2018} but have not, to our knowledge, been systematically applied to SBOM dependency graphs.

This dissertation presents SBOM Lens, a graph-based framework for software supply chain risk assessment. The system ingests Software Bills of Materials in CycloneDX format and constructs a directed dependency graph, over which graph-theoretic techniques are applied to surface structural vulnerabilities not visible through flat-inventory analysis. The design of SBOM Lens is described in \autoref{cp:system-design}, its vulnerability analysis in \autoref{cp:vuln-analysis} and its structural analysis in \autoref{cp:network-sci}, with empirical results presented in \autoref{cp:evaluation}.


\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

The SBOM analysis tools surveyed in \autoref{sec:sbom-tools} share a structural limitation that is architectural rather than incidental: they treat the dependency graph as a flat list. When a security analyst queries a tool such as Dependency-Track or grype for the vulnerabilities present in a given SBOM, the result is a table of (component, CVE, severity) tuples ranked by CVSS score, at most combined with exploit likelihood (EPSS) or known exploitation (KEV) --- measures of the vulnerability, not of where the component sits. Where the relationships between components are shown --- as Dependency-Track's dependency tree, which can be opened at the path to an affected component, or as Sunshine's sunburst and its table of dependents --- they are at most traversed, never analysed: there is no means of determining whether a vulnerable component sits at the centre of the dependency graph or at its periphery. This limitation has two concrete consequences for the analyst.

The first consequence is \textit{invisible blast radius}. When a new vulnerability is disclosed in a widely-used library --- as occurred with log4shell --- the analyst's immediate question is not ``does this component have a CVE?'' but rather ``how much of my system is exposed?'' Answering that question requires traversing the dependency graph from the vulnerable component back to all packages that depend on it, directly or transitively. Of the tools surveyed in \autoref{cp:related-work}, only Sunshine performs this traversal, and it reports the result as a list rather than on the graph; with the others, the analyst must do it manually, if they can do it at all.

The second consequence is \textit{undifferentiated prioritisation}. A CVSS score measures the intrinsic severity of a vulnerability in isolation --- attack vector, complexity, impact --- but says nothing about the structural importance of the affected component within the system. A critical-severity CVE in a component that is a leaf node with no dependents poses a fundamentally different remediation burden than the same CVE in a component that is a shared transitive dependency of dozens of packages throughout the graph. Without graph-structural context, both appear equally urgent on a flat list, and the analyst has no principled basis for deciding where to act first.

The literature points to this gap. A 2025 landscape study of 84 open-source SBOM tools \citep{Garcia2025} classifies them into generation, consumption, interoperability, quality assurance and services, and has no category for analysing the dependency structure an SBOM records: the tools that consume SBOMs for vulnerability management query databases such as NVD and report the vulnerable components they find. The survey of \autoref{cp:related-work} reaches the same conclusion for the most widely used tools. The closest existing tool is GUAC (Graph for Understanding Artifact Composition), developed under the OpenSSF umbrella, which constructs provenance graphs across multiple projects and organisations \citep{GUAC2024}. GUAC addresses supply chain transparency at a cross-project, ecosystem-wide scale, and can search the dependents of a vulnerable package, but it does not compute structural measures to assess the risk profile of a single software system. A detailed comparison of SBOM Lens with GUAC and other related tools is presented in \autoref{cp:related-work}.

SBOM Lens addresses this gap by constructing a directed dependency graph from a CycloneDX SBOM and providing the analyst with two complementary views. The first is \textit{impact traversal}: given any vulnerable component, the system identifies its blast radius --- the component and every component that depends on it, at any depth, as far as the SBOM records the dependencies --- together with the dependency paths through which it enters the application and its dominated set, the components that the application reaches only through it. The second is \textit{structural risk ranking}: using graph-theoretic measures, the system identifies which components are structurally most likely to propagate risk widely if compromised, allowing the analyst to prioritise monitoring and remediation based not only on CVE severity but on the component's position within the dependency graph. The question is not whether these capabilities can be built --- the system exists --- but what they reveal when applied to real-world open-source SBOMs, a question answered empirically in \autoref{cp:evaluation}.


\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

This work is framed as a design-and-analysis thesis: the system under study, SBOM Lens, has been built, and the objectives concern both its construction and what it reveals when applied empirically. Each objective maps directly to one of the implementation or evaluation chapters, allowing the conclusions drawn in \autoref{cp:conclusion} to be verified against each stated goal.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Graph construction pipeline.} To design and implement a directed dependency graph construction pipeline that ingests CycloneDX JSON SBOMs and produces an annotated graph --- with components as nodes and dependency relationships as directed edges --- suitable for interactive visualisation and further graph-theoretic analysis. (\autoref{cp:system-design})
    \item \textbf{Vulnerability integration.} To integrate vulnerability data from the Open Source Vulnerabilities database (OSV), which aggregates GitHub Security Advisories and other open-source advisory databases, with full CVSS v3.1 base score computation, and to overlay the results on the dependency graph so that the analyst can immediately see which nodes carry known vulnerabilities and at what severity, and, for any vulnerable component, to compute its blast radius, the dependency paths through which it enters the application, and its dominated set. (\autoref{cp:vuln-analysis})
    \item \textbf{Graph-based structural risk analysis.} To apply a suite of graph-theoretic algorithms --- including centrality and articulation point analysis to identify components with the highest structural propagation potential, and community detection to surface the modular structure of the application's dependency graph --- so as to give the analyst a principled basis for prioritising remediation beyond raw CVSS scores. (\autoref{cp:network-sci})
    \item \textbf{Empirical evaluation.} To evaluate the framework against the SBOMs of real-world open-source npm applications and demonstrate, through graph-level and node-level metrics, that the approach surfaces structural risk information that is not available through flat-inventory analysis. The evaluation asks what the dependency graphs of these applications look like (RQ1), whether a structural view of their vulnerable components orders them differently from the severity ranking a flat inventory provides (RQ2), and how much of their vulnerability exposure is hidden from a flat inventory (RQ3). (\autoref{cp:evaluation})
\end{enumerate}


\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This work makes four contributions, each corresponding to a distinct system capability or analytical result.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{SBOM Graph Visualisation.} We design and implement an interactive directed dependency graph constructed from CycloneDX JSON SBOMs and rendered using Cytoscape.js. The visualisation supports topology exploration, node inspection, and interactive editing of the dependency structure, providing the foundational data model upon which all subsequent analysis is performed. (\autoref{cp:system-design})
    \item \textbf{Vulnerability Analysis.} We implement a vulnerability integration pipeline that queries OSV by Package URL, deduplicates findings per component, and computes full CVSS v3.1 base scores. Vulnerable components are overlaid on the dependency graph and visually distinguished by severity band, giving the analyst an immediate picture of which nodes in the graph carry known vulnerabilities. For any vulnerable component, SBOM Lens computes its blast radius, up to five shortest dependency paths through which it enters the application, and its dominated set --- the components that the application reaches only through it. (\autoref{cp:vuln-analysis})
    \item \textbf{Graph-Based Structural Risk Analysis.} We apply a suite of graph-theoretic algorithms to the dependency graph to support analyst decision-making beyond raw CVE listings. Centrality analysis and articulation point detection identify the components whose structural position makes them candidates for closer monitoring, regardless of whether they currently carry a known CVE. Community detection reveals the modular structure of the application's dependency graph, supporting higher-level reasoning about isolation and blast containment. (\autoref{cp:network-sci})
    \item \textbf{Empirical Evaluation.} We evaluate the framework against a dataset of SBOMs of real-world open-source npm applications, collected reproducibly from public repositories. Through graph-level and node-level metrics we demonstrate that the approach surfaces structural risk information that is not available through flat-inventory analysis, and we interpret the results from an analyst's point of view. (\autoref{cp:evaluation})
\end{enumerate}


\section{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

This section defines the explicit boundaries of the work to prevent misreading of the claims made in subsequent chapters.

\textbf{SBOM format.} SBOM Lens supports CycloneDX JSON as its sole input format. SBOMs in SPDX or SWID formats are not supported. This is a deliberate design boundary rather than an oversight: CycloneDX states every dependency in a single directed form, it is the format produced by the generator used in the evaluation, and its structured \texttt{dependencies} array maps directly onto a directed graph without the need for format translation. Forward-compatibility with other formats is left as future work.

\textbf{Evaluation methodology.} The evaluation presented in \autoref{cp:evaluation} is metric-based: it reports graph-level and node-level measures computed on the production dependency graphs of 90 open-source npm applications, each with at least one critical known vulnerability (\autoref{sec:eval-dataset}), and its findings describe such applications. The thesis does not include a formal usability study, a user experiment, or any assessment of whether security analysts find the tool's output actionable in practice. Such a study would require ethical approval and a participant cohort that falls outside the scope of this work. The evaluation therefore shows whether the structural measures carry information that severity does not, not whether prioritising by them leads to better remediation.

\textbf{Features out of scope.} Three capabilities that naturally extend the SBOM Lens concept are explicitly excluded from this thesis. SBOM comparison and delta analysis --- the comparison of two SBOMs for the same project across time to detect dependency changes --- is a distinct analytical problem: SBOM Lens includes a pairwise comparison view (\autoref{sec:architecture-overview}), which is not evaluated, and tracking a project across versions would require a temporal data model not present in the current system. Automated restructuring of the dependency graph to reduce structural risk requires a constraint satisfaction or optimisation layer beyond the scope of this work; SBOM Lens's Topology Optimiser offers only heuristic suggestions derived from the spectral exposure score, which are described briefly in \autoref{sec:ns-optimizer} and are not evaluated. Finally, SBOM Lens operates on source-level SBOMs generated from package manifests and lockfiles; SBOMs derived from compiled binaries or container images raise format and toolchain questions that are not addressed here.

\textbf{Novelty claims.} This thesis does not claim that the graph-theoretic algorithms applied are themselves novel --- dominator trees, articulation point detection, centrality analysis, and community detection are well-established techniques. The contribution lies in their systematic application to SBOM dependency graphs as a coherent risk assessment framework, and in the empirical demonstration of what they reveal about real-world software supply chains.


\section{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:structure}

The remainder of this dissertation is organised as follows.

\autoref{cp:background} establishes the theoretical and technical foundations required to understand SBOM Lens. It covers the concept of a Software Bill of Materials and the CycloneDX format, the software supply chain security landscape, the fundamentals of graph theory and network science, and the vulnerability databases and scoring systems --- NVD, OSV, and CVSS v3.1 --- that underpin the vulnerability analysis.

\autoref{cp:related-work} surveys the existing landscape of SBOM analysis tools --- including Dependency-Track, grype, syft and Sunshine --- alongside graph-based security research and supply chain incident case studies. It identifies four gaps that, to our knowledge, current tooling and research leave open and that SBOM Lens addresses: no graph analysis in SBOM tools, no vulnerability impact in graph terms, no structural prioritisation beyond severity, and no graph analysis at the scale of a single application.

\autoref{cp:system-design} describes the architecture of SBOM Lens. It presents the React and FastAPI system design, the CycloneDX JSON parsing pipeline, the construction of the directed dependency graph, and the interactive SBOM Graph visualisation built with Cytoscape.js.

\autoref{cp:vuln-analysis} presents the vulnerability integration pipeline built on OSV and explains why OSV was chosen over querying NVD and GitHub Security Advisories directly. It details the CVSS v3.1 base score computation model, the vulnerability management workflow, the graph overlay that visually surfaces vulnerable nodes by severity band, and, for each vulnerable component, its blast radius, dependency paths and dominated set.

\autoref{cp:network-sci} presents the structural risk measures at three scales: per component (articulation points and bridges, HITS scores, the spectral exposure score, and core numbers), per cluster (Louvain communities, the bow-tie decomposition, and the rich-club coefficient), and for the whole graph (the spectral radius and epidemic threshold, degree entropy, cycles, version conflicts, namespace concentration, the degree distribution, and the small-world coefficient). For each technique, the chapter explains the theoretical basis and its interpretation in the context of software supply chain risk.

\autoref{cp:evaluation} evaluates the framework on the production dependency graphs of 90 open-source npm applications. It answers three research questions with graph-level and node-level metrics, reports the analysis time and the threats to validity, and discusses what the analysis reveals about structural risk in software supply chains.

\autoref{cp:conclusion} summarises the contributions of this work, maps them back to the research objectives stated in this chapter, acknowledges the limitations of the current implementation, and proposes directions for future research.
